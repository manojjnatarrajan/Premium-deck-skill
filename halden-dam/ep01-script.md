# Episode 1: I Guard a Dam That Isn't on Any Map. There Are 6 Rules.

**Narrator:** Night security guard. **Never says their name.**

## HOOK (0:00–0:20)

[ON SCREEN: black screen. Wind. Then a laminated card fades in, held under a flashlight. Six
numbered lines; line 7 is a black bar.]

You need to know six rules before you stand on Halden Dam at night.

But nobody tells you rule seven.

I've worked there for thirty-one nights. I lost the only friend I made there learning rule six. And
two nights ago I found out what rule seven is about.

## THE TURN (0:20–0:42)

[ON SCREEN: slow push-in on a gravel turnoff at night. A green mile marker: 57.]

So here's what I'm going to do. I'll give you the rules in the order I learned why they exist,
which isn't the order they're printed in. By the end you'll know everything I know about rule
seven, and you'll understand why I'm still going back.

It starts with a job listing that didn't have an address.

## BEAT 1: The listing (0:42–3:44)

[ON SCREEN: a phone screen in a dark room. A plain job post: "NIGHT SECURITY. Remote utility site.
$41/hr. Housing provided. Directions on acceptance."]

I'd been out of work for five months. I'd done night security before, at a distribution center
outside Missoula, the kind of job where the worst thing that happens is a raccoon in the loading bay.
So when I saw a listing for night security at a remote utility site paying forty-one dollars an
hour, I applied before I'd finished reading it.

The listing had no company name and no address. Under "location" it said, *Directions provided on
acceptance.* I remember thinking that was the kind of thing a scam would say. I applied anyway.

The call came the next morning. A man with a slow, careful voice, the kind of voice that sounds
like it's reading a contract to you. He said his name was Walter Ames and he was the night
supervisor. He asked three questions. Did I have a valid driver's license? Did I drink? And
could I keep a list of instructions exactly, even when they didn't make sense?

I said yes to the last one like it was a joke. He didn't laugh.

"Good," he said. "Most people can't."

He gave me the directions over the phone and asked me to write them down by hand. Not type them,
he said. *Write them.* Route 12 west out of town for an hour and ten minutes. At mile marker
fifty-seven there's a gravel turnoff with no sign. Take it. Follow the road down for nine miles.
Don't stop if you see someone walking. "You won't," he said, "but if you do, don't stop."

[ON SCREEN: headlights on a gravel road winding down through pines. Fog in the low ground.]

I drove up on a Sunday afternoon in April. The turnoff was exactly where he said. A gap in the
guardrail, a gravel road dropping into the trees, no sign, no gate. My phone lost signal about two
miles in. The road kept going down, switchback after switchback, and the air in the car got colder
in a way the heater couldn't fix.

Then the trees opened up and I saw the dam.

[ON SCREEN: a wide shot. A pale concrete wall curving between two mountainsides, a narrow road along
the top. Behind it, a long black lake.]

I'd looked it up before I drove out, obviously. Halden Dam. Halden Lake. I found the river on a
satellite map, but where the dam should have been, the image was just blurred, a smear of grey
where the valley should be. On every other map I checked, the river just ran through the valley
like there was nothing there at all.

But there it was. Four hundred feet of concrete holding back a lake about six miles long. A
powerhouse at the bottom. A guard hut on the crest, which is the road that runs along the top of
the dam. A cluster of old prefab staff housing on the hillside, and a little gravel lot with eleven
cars in it.

Mine made twelve.

## BEAT 2: Walter Ames and the card (3:44–6:59)

[ON SCREEN: a small guard hut interior. A 1950s steel desk, a rotary phone, a corkboard, a window
facing the lake with heavy metal shutters folded open.]

Walter Ames met me in the guard hut. I'd guessed from his voice that he'd be old, and he was:
somewhere in his seventies, tall, thin, in a grey work jacket buttoned all the way to the top. He
shook my hand, and his hand was cold. I'd been standing in an April wind for thirty seconds, so I
didn't think about it.

He didn't make small talk. He sat me down, put a laminated card on the desk between us, and slid it
across with one finger.

"These are yours," he said. "Everybody here gets their own. These are the ones for your job."

[ON SCREEN: the card, close up. Typewriter text, slightly faded under the laminate.]

I'm going to read them to you exactly as they're printed.

*HALDEN DAM — NIGHT SECURITY.*

*Rule one. Count the staff at every shift change. There are always twelve. If you count thirteen,
do not count again. Leave the room. Do not return until the next shift.*

*Rule two. Never say your name where you can see the water. On the radio you are badge 7-1-4.*

*Rule three. Walk the crest clockwise. Never counterclockwise.*

*Rule four. If the lake is still at night and there is no wind, close every shutter that faces the
water.*

*Rule five. If you hear church bells, step away from the railing. Walk. Never run.*

*Rule six. Nobody goes into the water after dark. If someone you know asks you to, it isn't them.*

And then there was a seventh line. The number seven was printed, and after it there was a solid
black bar. Not scratched out. It had been printed that way, a clean black rectangle, like a
redacted government document.

I asked him about it. Of course I did. Anybody would.

Ames looked at the card for a while. Then he said, "Seven isn't for your job," and that was all I
got.

I asked about rule four, the shutters. I asked what church bells he meant, because there was no
church for fifty miles. He waited until I was done and then said the thing I've thought about every
night since.

"You don't need to understand them. You need to do them. The people who get hurt here are the ones
who wait to understand."

Then he showed me the shift board.

[ON SCREEN: a corkboard by the door. Twelve brass hooks in two rows, each with a small painted
wooden tag. Each tag has a name and a badge number.]

It was a corkboard with twelve brass hooks on it. Every person on site had a wooden tag with their
name and badge number. When you came on shift, you hung your tag on a hook. When you left, you took
it with you. At every shift change the guard on duty, which was now me, counted the tags. "There are
always twelve," Ames said. "Always."

He handed me my tag. It was already painted. My name and *7-1-4*, in neat white letters, the
paint dry. I'd accepted the job thirty-six hours earlier.

I hung it on the twelfth hook. Ames watched me do it and nodded like I'd passed something.

## BEAT 3: The first week, and Ray (6:59–9:31)

[ON SCREEN: night on the crest. A flashlight beam on concrete, the railing, the lake below.]

The first week was the most normal week of work I've ever had.

The job is simple. I walk the crest every hour, clockwise. Out from the guard hut to the far
abutment, which is where the dam meets the mountain, then back along the downstream railing. It's
about a mile round trip. I check the gauges by the spillway gates, I check the doors to the
powerhouse stairs, and I log everything in a binder. Every two hours I radio the powerhouse, badge
to badge.

And every night at 10 p.m. and 6 a.m., at shift change, I count the tags on the board. Twelve.

I met most of the staff in that first week. They were friendly in a way I can only describe as
careful. People talked about the weather, the food, the drive up. Nobody talked about the rules,
and nobody said their name out loud on the crest. Indoors they'd introduce themselves normally.
The second they could see the lake, it was badge numbers.

The only one who talked to me like a normal person was Ray.

[ON SCREEN: a man's silhouette in a hi-vis jacket, leaning on the railing, a thermos in one hand.]

Ray was a maintenance tech, badge 4-0-2. He'd been at Halden for three years, longer than anyone
except Ames. He was about fifty, from Butte, big laugh, and he drank coffee so strong you could smell
it from the powerhouse door. He worked the swing shift, so our hours overlapped from ten to
midnight. Most nights he'd walk the first round with me.

"It's a good job," he told me on my second night. "Best money I ever made. You just do the card."

I asked him if he'd ever seen anything. He took a long time to answer, which I've learned is how
everyone at Halden answers that question.

"I've seen the lake go still," he said. "That's all I'll say about it out here."

On the sixth night I asked him about rule seven. He actually laughed.

"Everybody asks about seven. Ask Ames what's under the lake sometime. Watch what his face does."

I asked what's under the lake.

"A town," Ray said. "Halden. They flooded it in fifty-seven to make the reservoir. Church, school,
main street, all of it. Moved everybody out first." He looked down at the black water. "That's the
story anyway."

## BEAT 4: Rule two, the name (9:31–11:39)

[ON SCREEN: a handheld radio on the guard hut desk, its little red light on.]

The first rule I learned the reason for was rule two.

It was my eighth night. 2 a.m. I'd just finished a round and I was back in the hut writing up the
log, and the radio clicked on.

"Seven-one-four, this is the powerhouse."

It was Ames's voice. Slow and careful, exactly like on the phone.

I keyed the radio. "Go ahead."

"Seven-one-four, we've got a new system for the logbook. I need your full name for the
record, son."

And I almost said it.

I want to be honest about that. I had my thumb on the button and my name in my mouth, because it
was my boss asking a reasonable question at two in the morning, and I was tired. What stopped me
was the window.

[ON SCREEN: the hut window. Through it, the lake under a thin moon.]

I could see the water. Rule two: *Never say your name where you can see the water.*

So I said, "Badge seven-one-four, sir. Can it wait until I'm inside the housing block?"

The radio was quiet for a long time. Then the voice said, very gently, "That's all right. I'll ask
you again."

And then the door of the guard hut opened, and Walter Ames walked in.

He'd come up the stairs from the powerhouse, a ten-minute climb. He was out of breath. He had his
own radio clipped to his jacket, and its light was off.

"I didn't call you," he said, before I'd said anything. He was looking at the radio in my hand.
"Did you answer?"

I told him what I'd said. Badge number only.

He sat down in the other chair, which I'd never seen him do, and for a moment he looked every one of
his years. Then he said, "Good. If it asks again, it'll sound more like me each time. Don't answer
the radio after midnight unless you call first."

That wasn't on the card. I wrote it on the back in pen.

## BEAT 5: Rule four, the still water (11:39–14:29)

[ON SCREEN: the lake at night. Ripples moving across it in the wind. Then, slowly, the ripples
stop.]

Rule four happened on night eleven.

There's always wind at Halden. It comes down the valley and lifts the lake into little choppy
waves that slap against the upstream face of the dam all night. You stop hearing it after the first
week, the same way you stop hearing a fridge.

So I noticed when it stopped.

I was on the crest, about halfway out, walking clockwise. The slapping sound just faded out, and I
looked over the railing, and the lake was perfectly flat. Not calm. *Flat.* Like black glass. The
moon was on it with no ripple at all. And the strange part was, I could still feel the wind on my
face. The pines on the far shore were bending. It just wasn't touching the water.

Rule four. *If the lake is still at night and there is no wind, close every shutter that faces the
water.*

There was wind. But the water didn't care about the wind, and I decided that counted.

[ON SCREEN: hands pulling heavy metal shutters closed, one after another. Bolts sliding home.]

I walked back to the hut. Clockwise. I didn't run. The guard hut has four windows facing the lake,
each with a set of steel shutters that bolt from the inside. I closed them one by one and bolted them.
I radioed the powerhouse. I called first, like Ames said, and I said, "Seven-one-four. Still
water."

Ames came back immediately. "Copy. Stay inside until it moves."

So I sat in the hut with the shutters closed and the overhead light on, and I listened.

For about twenty minutes, nothing happened. And then I heard the sound that I still hear when I'm
falling asleep.

[ON SCREEN: the inside of a shutter. Silence. Then a slow, soft knock against the metal.]

Something was touching the shutters. Not knocking, exactly. It was the sound of a flat palm
pressing on metal, and then sliding. One window. Then the next one. Then the next. Like someone
was walking along the outside of the hut, trailing their hand along the glass, feeling for the
one that was open.

The guard hut is on the crest. On the lake side of those windows, there's a three-foot ledge,
then the railing, then a four-hundred-foot drop to the water.

It went along all four windows. Then it stopped. Then I heard the wind come back, and the slap of
little waves against the dam, and it was over.

When I opened the shutters at dawn, there were marks on the outside of the glass. Handprints. Small
ones, and wet, and they'd left a fine grey silt on the glass that took me ten minutes to scrub off.

Lake silt. From the bottom.

## BEAT 6: Rule five, the bells (14:29–17:05)

[ON SCREEN: the crest in fog. The railing fading into white ten feet ahead.]

By night nineteen, I'd convinced myself I had it handled. Do the card, like Ray said. The rules
work. I'd started to feel almost proud of it.

Then I heard the bells.

It was a foggy night, the kind of fog that sits right on the water and comes up over the railing
in slow sheets. I was on the far end of my round, close to the abutment, as far from the hut as
the crest goes. And from somewhere below me, I heard a bell.

One long, low note. Deep, like a church bell. It wasn't coming from the shore. It was coming from
*under* me, from the water, and it was muffled the way sound is muffled when your head is in the
bath.

Then another one. Then they started ringing properly, the way bells ring on a Sunday morning, a
whole peal of them rolling up out of the lake.

Rule five. *If you hear church bells, step away from the railing. Walk. Never run.*

[ON SCREEN: feet on concrete, moving steadily. The railing on the left. The fog shifting.]

I stepped away from the railing, to the downstream side of the road, and I started walking back
toward the hut. Clockwise. It's half a mile. Every part of my body wanted to run, and I want to
explain why I didn't, because it wasn't just the card.

It was the water.

The lake is on the left as you walk back. And as I walked, I could hear something in it, keeping
pace with me. A long, smooth sound, like something big moving just under the surface. When I
walked faster, it went faster. When I slowed down, it slowed down. It was matching me, step for
step, about four hundred feet below.

And I understood, the way you understand things in dreams, that it was waiting for me to run.
That running was how it would know I was afraid. That being afraid was a kind of permission.

So I walked. I walked half a mile at a normal pace with church bells ringing under my feet and
something swimming beside me, and I didn't run, and I didn't look over the railing.

The bells stopped when I touched the guard hut door. All at once, mid-note. Like someone had
cut a wire.

I sat down on the floor of the hut, and I didn't move until shift change.

## BEAT 7: Rule six, Ray (17:05–20:09)

[ON SCREEN: the lake at night, very still. A single figure standing on the water, far out, its back
turned.]

This is the part that's hard to tell.

It was night twenty-six. Ray and I were doing the first round together, like always, ten o'clock.
The lake was choppy and normal. We were talking about the Grizzlies game.

And then Ray stopped walking.

He was looking out over the railing at the lake. I followed his eyes and I saw her.

A woman was standing on the water. About two hundred yards out. Not swimming. Not on a boat.
*Standing*, on the surface, the way you'd stand on a sidewalk, with the little waves moving around
her ankles. She had her back to us. She was wearing a dress from another time, long and dark. And
her hair was wet, and hanging straight down.

Ray made a sound I'd never heard a grown man make.

"That's Carol," he said.

I didn't know who Carol was. I found out later, from his file in the office, that Carol was his
wife. She died in 2019. A car accident on the interstate, a long way from Halden.

[ON SCREEN: the woman on the water, slowly turning to face the dam.]

She turned around. I couldn't see her face from that far, but Ray could, or thought he could. And
she raised one hand and waved. Then she called out, and her voice came across that water as clear
as if she was standing next to us.

"*Ray. Come in, the water's warm.*"

I grabbed his arm. I said it out loud, the whole rule. "Nobody goes into the water after dark.
If someone you know asks you to, it isn't them." I said it twice. I said, "Ray, look at me, that
isn't her," and he did look at me, and he nodded. He said, "I know." He said, "I know it isn't."

And then he said, "But what if it's what's left of her?"

I held on to his arm all the way back to the hut. I made him sit down. I radioed Ames and said
the words "rule six" and Ames was there in eight minutes, which should be impossible from the
powerhouse. He sat with Ray, and he told me to finish my rounds, and I did.

When I came back at midnight, they were both gone.

Ames was down at the powerhouse; he said Ray had gone back to the housing block to sleep. I
believed him. I wanted to.

At 6 a.m., at shift change, Ray's tag wasn't on the board.

I counted eleven tags. Eleven. The card says there are always twelve, and it didn't say what to do
about eleven. I called Ames. He was quiet for a long time, and then he said, "Badge four-oh-two has
left the site." That's all he'd say.

Ray's car was still in the lot. It's still there now.

## BEAT 8: Rule one, the count (20:09–22:13)

[ON SCREEN: the shift board. Twelve hooks. The camera slowly counts across them.]

Four nights later, at 10 p.m. shift change, I counted the board, like always.

Twelve.

Then my eye caught on the twelfth hook, my hook, and there were two tags on it.

Mine, with my name and *7-1-4*. And behind it, another one. Older. The paint had yellowed, and
the wood was dark and soft, like it had been wet for a long time. I lifted it with one finger.

It had my name on it. My full name, the one I've never said within sight of that lake, in
careful white letters. And under the name, where the badge number should be, it said *1957*.

Thirteen tags.

Rule one. *If you count thirteen, do not count again. Leave the room.*

[ON SCREEN: a door closing. Footsteps on gravel. The hut's lit window from outside, getting
smaller.]

I didn't count again. I let go of the tag. I walked out of that hut, and I closed the door behind
me, and I stood on the gravel by the cars until 6 a.m. with my flashlight on and my back to the
water.

When I went back in at shift change, there were twelve tags on the board. My tag was on the
twelfth hook, alone. The old one was gone.

And Ray's tag was back.

Hanging on the fourth hook, where it always was, the paint dry and clean.

At 10 that night, Ray walked into the guard hut with his thermos and said, "Ready?" He smiled at
me. It was his smile, exactly. And we went out on the crest to do the first round.

He started walking counterclockwise.

I stood still. He walked about ten steps, then stopped, turned, and looked at me. For a long second
nothing on his face moved at all. Then he laughed, Ray's big laugh, and said, "Habit," and
came back and walked the right way.

I don't know what was in that thermos. I couldn't smell coffee.

## PAYOFF: What rule seven is about (22:13–24:07)

[ON SCREEN: a dusty filing cabinet in a back office. A framed black-and-white photo, face down.]

I promised you I'd tell you what I know about rule seven. Here it is.

Two nights ago, Ames sent me to the back office in the guard hut to find a spare key. It's a
little storeroom full of filing cabinets nobody's opened in decades. In the bottom drawer of the
last cabinet, under a stack of old maintenance logs, there was a framed photograph.

[ON SCREEN: the photo. Black and white. A crowd of workmen in front of the half-built dam. A
banner: HALDEN DAM — DEDICATION — APRIL 1957.]

It's the dedication of the dam, April 1957. About forty men in work clothes and hard hats,
standing in front of the concrete, squinting into the sun. In the front row, right in the
middle, in a grey work jacket buttoned to the top, is Walter Ames.

He isn't younger in the photo. He's the same age. Same face, same lines, same jacket.

And he's holding something up to the camera, the way you'd hold up a certificate. It's a card.
I brought a magnifying glass from the desk. It's the same card I carry: same typewriter text,
same layout. But on his card there are seven rules, and none of them are blacked out.

I can't read the words. The photo's too grainy. But under the photo, someone had written a
caption on the matting in pencil:

*"Reservoir full. Elevation 4,112. Steeple under. Rule 7 holds."*

[ON SCREEN: the pencil caption, close up.]

So that's what I know. Rule seven is about the water level. It's about something that's under the
lake, something with a steeple. And the man who wrote the rules has been here since the day the
water went over Halden, and he hasn't aged a day.

I put the photo back exactly where I found it. I didn't ask Ames about it. I think he knows I saw
it anyway.

## CLOSE (24:07–24:57)

[ON SCREEN: the crest at dawn. Pale light. A lone figure walking clockwise.]

I'm still working at Halden. It pays forty-one dollars an hour, and I've done the math on what
happens if I leave, and I can't make it work. But that's not really why I stay. I stay because
somebody has to count the board.

Next week a new diver starts at the dam. Her job is to go down the intake shaft at night and check
the grates. I saw her paperwork in the office. I hope somebody gives her the right card.

If you want to hear what she finds down there, subscribe. Her story goes up next week.

And if you ever find a job listing with no address, just directions, don't say your name out
loud once you can see the water.

[ON SCREEN: black. Then the card again, under the flashlight, rule 7's black bar. Fade out.]
