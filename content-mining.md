# Content Mining — Turning Research Into a Deck That Earns Its Slides

The single biggest gap between a mediocre AI-built deck and a genuinely sharp one is not design. It's whether the deck actually uses what the research found, or just gestures at the topic the research covered. A deck can reference "regulatory pressure" and "competitive gaps" in vague terms, or it can say "CBAM penalty exposure of €1.05–2.6M annually for a mid-sized steel importer" and name exactly which two competitors can't do product-level tracing and why. The second one is a different category of document, built from identical source material.

## Before writing a single slide, extract these five things from the research

1. **Every number that has a unit attached to it.** Market sizes, prices, percentages, dates, deal values, penalty exposure, headcounts, plant counts, patent numbers. If the research contains "£8,000–£18,000 per year" or "50,000 companies" or "January 2026," those figures are the deck's raw material — not the paragraph of prose around them.
2. **Every named competitor, paired with its specific, sourced weakness.** Not "competitors lack our capabilities" — which competitor, and what exactly can't it do. "Watershed and Persefoni operate at organisational-level aggregation — they can report a company's annual Scope 3 footprint but cannot certify a specific production batch's embedded carbon, which is precisely what CBAM declarants need" is usable. "Other tools are less comprehensive" is not.
3. **The single sharpest quote-worthy sentence per research section.** Good research usually has one sentence per section that crystallizes the finding — often near a section's opening or closing. Find it and either use it close to verbatim or build the slide's headline around its claim.
4. **Whatever answers "why does this matter to the money."** Every well-built research report ties its facts back to commercial consequence — cost of inaction, revenue opportunity, deal shape. That's not background; that's pitch-deck content, usually deserving its own slide.
5. **Anything that is true of Goenvi specifically and could not be said by a competitor.** Patent numbers, grant amounts, plant counts, accreditations, proprietary integrations (e.g. CTDT feeding directly into dMRV). These are the credibility anchors — a deck with zero company-specific proof points reads as a concept pitch, not a real company.

## The failure mode to actively avoid

Writing a plausible-sounding SaaS deck that could describe almost any product in the category, using the research only to confirm the general subject matter rather than mining it for specifics. This happens when a slide gets written from "what would a slide about competitive positioning generally say" instead of "what did the research specifically find about these five named competitors." The tell is genericness that survives even when real research was available — if a slide could have been written without reading the source material, it was.

## A concrete test before finalizing any slide

Ask: **could this exact slide appear in a deck for a different company in a different category, with just the product name swapped?** If yes, it needs a specific number, a named entity, or a sourced claim added before it ships. A comparison slide that says "Legacy tools use estimates; we use real data" fails this test. One that says "Watershed and Sweep rely on spend-based Scope 3 estimates at $50k–$300k+ ACV; CETrace ingests transaction-level data at £8k–£120k, with no enterprise lock-in" passes it.

## Where this content belongs

Numbers and named comparisons are usually strong enough to justify a slide of their own rather than being folded into general bullets — a big-stat layout for a single sharp figure, a comparison layout for a named competitor breakdown, a roadmap layout for a phased go-to-market sequence with real dates. Don't bury a €1M+ penalty-exposure figure in a supporting bullet on a slide about something else; it's compelling enough to anchor its own slide.
