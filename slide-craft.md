# Slide Craft — Practical Heuristics

## Headlines make a claim, not a topic label

A weak headline names the subject: "Market Overview," "Competitive Landscape," "Our Solution." A strong headline makes the specific claim that slide exists to prove: "Five Regulations. One Data Problem. One Deadline." or "CETrace vs. The Market — Where Every Competitor Breaks Down." The reader should be able to understand the deck's argument from the headlines alone, without reading a single bullet. If a headline could sit unchanged above completely different content, it's a topic label, not a claim — rewrite it.

## One idea per slide, genuinely

A slide trying to make two separate points usually makes neither well. If a slide's bullets are covering two distinct arguments (e.g. "why now" and "why us" on the same slide), that's two slides, not an efficiency. The tell: if you can't summarize the slide's single point in one sentence without using "and" to join two unrelated claims, split it.

## Choosing a layout: let the content pick it, not the slide count

- **big_stat** — one number is doing more work than any sentence could. Use when the research has a figure genuinely striking enough to anchor a whole slide (penalty exposure, market size, a deployment count, a patent number's real significance).
- **comparison** — two things are being directly weighed against each other with real asymmetry (us vs. them, before vs. after, legacy vs. modern). Both sides need genuinely comparable, specific content — not one detailed side and one vague side.
- **roadmap** — there's a real sequence with genuine order-dependency (a rollout, a phased go-to-market, an onboarding flow). Don't use it for a list that has no actual order.
- **quote** — a single sentence is strong enough to stand completely alone with no supporting bullets. If it needs bullets around it to make sense, it's not quote material yet — tighten it first.
- **bullets** — the default for a genuine list of distinct, parallel points. Cap at four or five; a bullet list beyond that is usually two slides pretending to be one.
- **image_split** — a real visual (product screenshot, diagram, photo) adds information text can't — not decoration for its own sake. If there's no genuinely relevant image available, don't force this layout; fall back to bullets or big_stat.
- **section_divider** — marks a genuine shift in the deck's argument (moving from "the problem" to "the solution"), not just a slide count throat-clear between similar content.
- **closing_cta** — the explicit ask needs to be a real, specific action ("book a 90-day pilot," "start your CBAM readiness assessment") — not a vague "let's talk" that gives the reader nothing concrete to say yes to.

## Cutting is part of the craft

A research report will always contain more than a deck needs. The instinct to include everything researched, spread thin across more slides, produces a worse deck than a tighter one built from the strongest third of the material. If two findings support the same underlying point, use the stronger one and cut the other rather than giving both their own slide. A 9-slide deck where every slide earns its place beats a 14-slide deck where half the slides are restating an earlier point in different words.

## The founder's actual ask belongs on its own slide, stated plainly

Whatever the deck is actually asking for — investment, a pilot, a meeting, a signature — deserves a dedicated closing slide that states it as a specific, concrete action, not folded into a generic "thank you" close. A reader should be able to say, from that one slide alone, exactly what happens next if they say yes.
